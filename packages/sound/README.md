# @apendua/sound

The noises the demos make. Internal to this repo, and not published.

`@apendua.dev/media` is the other half of the same subject and holds the files — the soundtracks the
apps stream. This one holds the sounds that are cheaper to compute than to fetch, and the three.js
plumbing that puts them somewhere in the room rather than in the middle of your head.

```
rush build --to @apendua/sound
```

JavaScript with JSDoc types, checked by `tsc` and shipped as source, like `signal`, `justdom` and
`freedom`; the `.d.ts` files under `lib-types` are generated by `build:types` and are what a
consumer sees.

There are two entry points. `@apendua/sound` is the waveforms, the voices and the output picker, and
needs nothing but three.js. `@apendua/sound/xr` is the soundtrack player, which is tied to the life
of an XR session and so needs `justdom-three` — an optional peer, kept behind its own subpath so
that a consumer of the first half never has to have it.

## What is in it

**`createClickWaveform(sampleRate)`** — fifty milliseconds of knock with a little ring left in it,
the way two magnets sound when they meet: a scrape of contact noise on the leading edge, two
partials ringing under it, faded at both ends so it cannot pop. It returns a `Float32Array` ready
for `AudioBuffer.copyToChannel` and touches no Web Audio API at all, which is why it is tested in
Node. The noise comes from a seeded generator, so the click is the same sound in every session and
can be asserted on.

**`createClicks(listener, options?)`** — the same click, in the room. It hands back an object to add
to the scene and a `play(position)` that sounds a click at a point in _that object's_ coordinates:

```js
const clicks = createClicks(listener);
// Inside the board, so a click sounds from the cell it belongs to.
board.add(clicks.object);
// ...on landing a brick:
clicks.play(cellCentre);
```

Add it to the board rather than to the scene: a board is usually scaled and parked at arm's length,
and the positions the game already has are in its coordinates, not the room's.

`options` covers the three things an app is likely to disagree about — `voices`, `refDistance`,
`volume` — and the defaults suit a board about a metre square within arm's reach, which is every app
here so far.

**`createVoices(listener, samples, options?)`** is the same, for any waveform. `createClicks` is
`createVoices` with the click in it. The options are the same, and `play(position, loudness?)` takes
a second argument from 0 to 1 that scales that one note, for a sound that is louder the harder its
event was.

**`createSweepWaveform(sampleRate, sweep)`** is one frequency slide (`from` and `to` in Hz, over
`duration` seconds), dying away at `decay`, with `edge` of second harmonic on it and `noise` of
contact on its onset. A slide up is a pluck and a slide down is a thump. Like the click it returns
samples peaking at exactly one and touches no Web Audio API.

**`checkSampleRate`, `createNoise` and `normalize`** are what the click and the sweep are both made
of: the rate check, the seeded noise, and the pass that makes a waveform's loudest sample one. They
are exported for an app writing a noise of its own.

**`audioOutputSelection()`** — which speakers all of this comes out of. A desktop headset is rarely
the only thing plugged in, and the browser's own choice is not always the one the player is wearing:

```js
const output = audioOutputSelection();
// output.outputs — everything sound could be sent to, as a signal
// output.deviceId — the chosen one, empty until the player picks
// output.select(id) — send the sound somewhere else
// output.updates — hold it open (use) to keep the list current
```

`setAudioOutput(listener.context, deviceId)` applies the choice to an `AudioListener`'s context, and
`audioOutputs()` is the list on its own for anything that wants it without the selection around it.

## What is in `/xr`

**`createMusicControls({ track, volume? })`** — the soundtrack's state, in one object: the volume as
a signal, an `audioOutputSelection()` of its own, and a `step` that moves the volume by whole tenths
and stops at silence and at full. The track is a URL, which in every app here is an asset import out
of `@apendua.dev/media`. Volume starts at `DEFAULT_MUSIC_VOLUME` (0.5) unless the app says
otherwise.

**`Music(ctx, listener, controls)`** — plays it. The track starts when an XR session begins and
stops when it ends, which keeps it out of the way of autoplay policy and leaves the browser tab
silent until the headset goes on. Volume and output are followed live, so turning the music down
takes the gain of the track that is playing rather than starting it over from the top.

```js
const listener = new THREE.AudioListener();
const music = createMusicControls({ track: glass_desk_habits, volume: 0.35 });
// ...in the scene, with the listener on the camera:
Music(ctx, listener, music);
// ...and on a panel somewhere: music.volume.get(), music.step(-1), music.output
```

Held apart like this because the two ends are rarely near each other: the music starts wherever the
scene is built, while the panel that turns it down is often several routes away, so both reach it
through the controls object — passed down, or put in a context, as `marblerun` does.

## Three things worth knowing

**Several voices.** One `PositionalAudio` node cannot play two clicks at once, and placing bricks
quickly is the whole game. Four voices are used in turn, so a click only ever cuts off one that is
already several placements old.

**One entry per physical output.** Chromium names the same speakers up to three times — as
`default`, as `communications`, and under their own name — so the list folds those into the device
they point at. That is what lets a scene decide whether to offer a switch by asking whether there is
more than one entry, and show nothing on a headset with one way out for sound. Where the platform
cannot route audio at all — `AudioContext.setSinkId` is Chromium-only — the list is empty rather
than a switch that does nothing.

**The panner is placed after `play()`.** three.js only moves a positional sound's panner while the
sound is playing, and reads it as the note starts. Setting the position and waiting for the next
frame would put the click wherever the _last_ one landed.

## Used by

`castle-xr` and `marblerun` on a brick coming to rest, and `polytope-xr` on two clusters snapping
together — which is the same event wearing a different hat, and used to be a `Switch1.wav` loaded by
hand in that app.

`pinball` makes all of its noises with `createSweepWaveform` and `createVoices`, and uses the click
for the ball meeting a wall or a flipper. `hexboard-xr`, `laser-xr` and `paperwings-xr` each carry
an older copy of the sweep and of the voice pool, written before the package had them, and
`pirates-xr` carries a voice pool and a variant of the sweep with its own noise decay and smoothing.

The soundtrack player and the output picker are used by every app here with music, the three above
among them, each behind a Music section on a panel it already had. Every one of them had its own
copy of the player before this package took it; `knights` still has its own, and should, being a
flat page with an `<audio>` element rather than a session to hang the track on.

Not `bricks-xr`: that one is a worked example, kept to the smallest thing that still builds
something, and a sound is not part of what it is there to show.
